Coding is the tool in this experience. Career exploration is the outcome. Nine people work on the race team students join, and each one is attached to a decision the student actually makes rather than to a slide about jobs.

This page holds what the tutorials do not have room for: how each of these people got into the work, and one question you can ask the room when their stage ends.

## Where Each Role Appears

Each stage opens with one mentor and closes with a line that ties the decision the student just made back to that role.

| Stage | Opens with | Closes with | The decision it is attached to |
|---|---|---|---|
| Join the Team | Kai, Operations Lead | Drew, UX Designer | how the dashboard reads |
| Design | Riley, Performance Engineer | Riley | what more speed will cost |
| Test | Jordan, Test Engineer | Jordan | which single variable to change |
| Analyze | Casey, Telemetry Analyst | Casey | what counts as a result worth keeping |
| Decide | Morgan, Strategist | Avery, Sustainability Lead | when to pit, and what a stop is worth |
| Race and Reflect | Taylor, Systems Engineer | all nine | which role the student's own decisions matched |

Sam, the software engineer, is the build lens rather than a stage lens. Sam voices the hints that explain how the tutorial itself works, and software engineer is one of the four role lenses a student selects in code during Design.

## The Four Role Lenses in the Game

In Design, students choose a role lens. The garage test bench then reads the same result back to them differently depending on the choice:

- performance engineer: what ten more speed would save per lap, and whether it is worth the energy
- strategist: what the pit stops cost in seconds, and whether fewer stops beat faster laps
- software engineer: whether the speed and cost values match the rule the student wrote
- data analyst: how many laps a tank lasts, and how many refills twenty laps therefore needs

The point is not that one lens is right. It is that four people can read one result and each notice something true.

## The Nine Roles

### Kai, Operations Lead

On a real team, Kai coordinates timelines, makes sure resets go smoothly, and builds handoffs so the next shift can pick up where the last one left off.

Kai came in the hands-on way: setting up equipment, keeping things running, and learning systems by doing them, then adding project planning and process design later.

**Ask:** Kai says reliability is not luck, it is built. What in this room runs reliably because somebody set it up that way?

### Drew, UX Designer

Drew watches for the exact moment someone hesitates. A pause, a wrong click, a "what am I supposed to do now" is design data, and Drew turns it into clearer prompts and simpler next steps.

Drew started self-taught, watching tutorials and redesigning menus in their own projects, then added a formal design class and a great deal of play testing.

**Ask:** Where did you hesitate today? Drew would call that data, not a mistake.

### Sam, Software Engineer

Sam writes and tests control code, fixes unexpected behavior, and keeps the dashboard reliable.

Sam got into coding by remixing games and following tutorials, with no formal degree at first, then added structured courses and certifications after getting more serious. That mix of self-taught and structured is the path, not a detour from it.

**Ask:** Sam started by remixing somebody else's game. What would you remix first?

### Riley, Performance Engineer

Riley runs A/B tests, turns driver feedback into data, and tunes the car so it is both fast and controllable.

Riley studied physics and math in college, and is quick to say that performance engineers also arrive through technical programs, apprenticeships, and the military.

**Ask:** Riley's whole job starts with one question: why did that change make it better, or worse? What did you change today that you cannot yet explain?

### Jordan, Test Engineer

Jordan turns "I think the car handles well" into "I know it does, and here is the data."

Jordan did not start out coding. The route was hands-on troubleshooting, and learning to document a problem clearly long before automating any test.

**Ask:** What did running twice prove that running once could not?

### Casey, Telemetry Analyst

Casey cleans messy data, builds simple dashboards, and helps engineers answer one question: did that change actually help?

Casey started by tracking game and sports statistics for fun, then picked up spreadsheets and basic coding through a community college data course.

**Ask:** Casey's job is that one question. What in your session answered it, and what only felt like an answer?

### Morgan, Strategist

Morgan monitors live conditions, weighs the safer option against the riskier one, and helps the team make fast calls when everyone holds a different piece of the picture.

Morgan started in track operations, learning timing and logistics by doing the work and talking with the engineers and analysts nearby.

**Ask:** Morgan has to call it before anyone knows how it ends. When did you commit today without knowing?

### Avery, Sustainability Lead

Avery maps the ripple effects of design choices on energy, materials, heat, and safety, and keeps the environment in the conversation when conditions shift.

Avery came through a college engineering program, but says the hook was not the textbooks. It was realizing that every design choice ripples outward.

**Ask:** Every pit stop spends something. What did your strategy spend, and what did it buy?

### Taylor, Systems Engineer

Taylor does integration testing: connecting the subsystems and checking that the whole experience works, not just one part in isolation.

Taylor got started in a two-year electronics program, learning by doing, then kept building through coursework and certifications while already working in the field.

**Ask:** Which of your systems broke first under pressure? That is the one Taylor would test next.

## Three Ways In

Read across the nine and the same point keeps surfacing, which is the one worth making explicit to students:

- self-taught and project-based: Sam and Drew both started by remixing and redesigning things they already used
- two-year and community programs: Taylor through electronics, Casey through a community college data course
- four-year degrees: Riley through physics and math, Avery through engineering

Kai, Jordan, and Morgan all came in through hands-on operations work and grew technical skill on the job. None of these routes is the exception.

## Using This Page

- read one mentor's route aloud when that stage ends, while the decision is still fresh
- use the **Ask** line as the closing question for that stage rather than a general "what did you learn"
- when a student says they are not a coder, Sam and Drew are the two to name first
- save the full roll call for the end of Race and Reflect, where the game names all nine and shows the student which version of each job they did
